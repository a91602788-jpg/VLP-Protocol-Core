# Routing, relay trust, and privacy research

**Status:** research candidates and proposed evaluation questions. **Source review date:** 2026-10-19. This note selects no routing architecture, cryptographic construction, dependency, or deployment profile. It contains no VLP measurements or gate outcome.

The current [decision process](../process/decision-process.md) remains **Research → Research Gate → VLP-0001 / VLP-0002 / VLP-0003 → Specification Review → Implementation Approval**. The three identifiers remain reserved for future formal specifications. This document does not define a wire format or authorize implementation, prototypes, or packet-generating experiments.

## Research direction

The useful comparison is between explicit privacy goals and their costs. A fast encrypted connection, a connection forwarded through an untrusted relay, an anonymous communication path, and a delayed private message service have different trust boundaries. Combining their names does not establish a secure composition.

The proposed research order is to establish the cost and security model of a direct connection between authenticated endpoints, then assess a relay that forwards an independently protected endpoint channel. Layered routing should be evaluated against an identified observer and collusion model. Asynchronous mixing should be evaluated as a message-service alternative with its own delivery deadlines. This is a research sequencing proposal, not adoption of a mandatory baseline or deferral of any requirement without review.

The labels `DIRECT`, `E2E_RELAY`, `LAYERED`, and `ASYNC_MIX` below identify comparison models only. They are not negotiated values, product modes, or accepted VLP profiles.

## What protection ends where?

**Cryptographic endpoints** are the parties that hold the keys needed to recover application content. In a phone-to-service connection, the service can legitimately be an endpoint and read the request. If the intended recipients are two phones and an intermediary service must remain unable to read messages, encryption must continue to those phones. Calling a server a relay does not change the consequences of giving it content-decryption keys.

**Hop protection** authenticates and protects a connection between adjacent participants. If each relay decrypts the entire payload and re-encrypts it for its neighbor, all those relays are trusted for payload confidentiality. **Endpoint protection** instead leaves an inner authenticated channel opaque to forwarding relays. It still needs endpoint identity verification, key lifecycle rules, replay handling, and binding to the intended session and peer. More forwarding hops do not establish forward secrecy or recovery after key compromise; those are separate cryptographic questions.

A **transit relay** forwards traffic toward another participant under a routing policy. An **exit** additionally connects the overlay to an external destination. An exit necessarily obtains enough destination information to perform that function; application content remains protected beyond it only if encryption reaches the actual application endpoint. The original Sphinx construction explicitly distinguishes forwarding through mixes from final delivery to an external recipient, illustrating why packet-layer protection alone is insufficient for that last segment. [R4] (§3)

**Camouflage** concerns what an observer can infer about the traffic's protocol or shape. **Anonymity and unlinkability** concern relationships between participants, actions, sessions, or messages. Endpoint encryption concerns content and authenticity. Each needs its own claim, adversary, and evidence. A packet that is difficult to classify can still be correlated with another flow; an anonymous path can still carry identifiable application content.

## Four comparison models

The following table is VLP research analysis under deliberately stated assumptions. It describes information available by role, not behavior demonstrated by an implementation. “Sees an address” refers to the address on the observed network segment; an upstream NAT or proxy can change that address without eliminating the relationship being observed.

| Model and assumed route | Keys and trust boundary | Metadata visible to intermediaries and endpoints | Residual exposure and research obligation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **DIRECT:** endpoint A communicates with endpoint B | A and B authenticate each other and hold content keys. There is no VLP forwarding relay. | B sees A's connecting address and whatever identity the application supplies. Observers of the connection see its network endpoints, timing, direction, and sizes. | This is a confidentiality and transport baseline. Hiding the communicating pair is not an assumed property. Endpoint compromise and application identifiers remain outside protection from network relays. |
| **E2E_RELAY:** A communicates with B through one transit relay | A and B hold inner content keys. The relay may terminate separate outer channels for admission and forwarding, but does not receive inner keys. Endpoint authentication cannot depend solely on an unauthenticated key supplied by that relay. | In the simple forwarding model, the relay can associate A's incoming connection with B's outgoing connection and observe timing and volume. B sees the relay's connecting address, unless an additional field discloses A's address; application authentication can still identify A. | An untrusted relay can be excluded from content trust while still learning the communicating pair. It can also delay, drop, replay, or deny service. Additional separation of the two address views would be a different architecture and collusion model. |
| **LAYERED:** A reaches B through several relays with separate forwarding layers | Assume inner A-to-B content protection plus appropriately authenticated per-hop forwarding layers. Directory and path selection determine which parties participate. | The entry sees A and its next hop; an intermediate relay normally sees adjacent hops; the final relay sees the final delivery target or next service. An exit sees the external destination. Fields or control services can disclose more than this minimal model. | Splitting address knowledge across relays depends on their independence and the absence of identifying side information. Colluding entry/exit observers, a common operator, or an observer across relevant links can reconnect the views. Extra hops alone do not defeat traffic correlation. |
| **ASYNC_MIX:** messages pass through a mixing service, potentially using sender/recipient providers or mailboxes | Assume recipient content encryption plus a reviewed mix packet construction. Providers, mixes, scheduling, admission, cover traffic, and recipient retrieval are part of the privacy model. | A sender provider can associate a client with submission times; a recipient provider can associate a mailbox with delivery or retrieval activity. Individual mixes see their adjacent connections and local processing. Application content may reveal identities to the recipient. | Delay, cover, honest participation, and observation assumptions determine what linkage is hidden. Being asynchronous does not require offline endpoints or fixed batch rounds. Delivery receipts, retries, sparse activity, or stable mailboxes may create linkability and need separate analysis. |

The final endpoint is not automatically an exit: a VLP server can itself be the intended application destination. Conversely, a service that decrypts the inner channel and forwards plaintext is a cryptographic endpoint even if the product describes it as infrastructure.

## What the proposed techniques actually contribute

The entries are prior art at different layers. They are references for reasoning, not selected dependencies or claims that their security properties compose unchanged in VLP.

| Candidate or family | Relevant contribution from the primary source | Boundary for VLP research |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Tor / onion routing** | Tor separates adjacent encrypted channels from circuits with per-relay cryptographic layers. Its documented client service is TCP oriented; the SOCKS specification does not support `UDP ASSOCIATE`. [R1] | Study circuit trust, relay selection, and stream behavior. This is not evidence that native VLP UDP datagrams can use the documented SOCKS service unchanged. |
| **I2P / garlic routing** | The official overview uses garlic terminology for layering and message bundling, and describes unidirectional tunnels with application-destination protection above them. [R2] | Bundling is not inherently stronger cryptography. Evaluate batching, delivery instructions, and metadata separately. The overview includes legacy cryptographic terminology and is not a current cipher-suite selection source. |
| **HORNET** | The paper investigates packet-carried forwarding state and symmetric processing at intermediate nodes, with paths and verifiable router keys available to the sender. [R3] (§§2, 4) | Research the state/packet-size and setup/forwarding tradeoffs. Stateless forwarding does not remove key management, admission, replay, or infrastructure obligations. It is not a drop-in Internet deployment design. |
| **Sphinx** | A cryptographic message format for routing through mixes, with forward/reply handling and security arguments in a stated model. [R4] (§§2–4) | A packet construction does not supply the directory, mixing schedule, cover policy, transport reliability, or confidential external delivery. A historical proof is not a review of a new VLP composition. |
| **Mixnets / Loopix** | Loopix combines a mix architecture, independently sampled delays, cover messages, and providers. Its model distinguishes observation from corruption and constrains both. [R5] (§§2–4) | Study private messaging with a delay and bandwidth budget. The cited model includes global passive observation but restricts corrupted providers to honest-but-curious behavior and excludes Sybil attacks through its participant assumptions. Those limitations must accompany any comparison. |
| **DC-nets / Dissent** | Dissent studies anonymous group communication using a client/server architecture, coordinated participation, and disruption-handling mechanisms. Its anytrust assumption requires an honest server within the relevant server set. [R6] (§3) | This is a group communication construction, not a fast route-selection algorithm. Membership, scheduling, churn, accountability, and confidential recipient delivery are separate obligations. A large group is not automatically a large honest anonymity set. |
| **Dandelion / Dandelion++** | Dandelion++ studies hiding the origin of broadcast transactions using a stem phase followed by broader dissemination, under an explicit network and adversary model. [R7] (§§2–4) | Broadcast-origin privacy is distinct from a confidential bidirectional tunnel. Consider it only for a justified future announcement/gossip use case; it does not establish encrypted transport or recipient anonymity. |
| **F2F / OneSwarm** | OneSwarm studies sharing over an overlay with explicit trust choices. Its threat model does not guarantee privacy against an adversary observing or modifying arbitrary network links. [R8] (§§3–4) | Friend-based admission and mesh topology influence reachability and trust. Known neighbors and social relationships can also be metadata. Neither F2F nor “mesh” specifies a cryptographic protocol by itself. |
| **DHT / Kademlia** | Kademlia defines key/value lookup and node discovery using an XOR metric and contact information. [R9] (§§1–2) | A lookup structure belongs to discovery/control research. It does not by itself authenticate a service, establish operator independence, or provide private queries and anonymous payload delivery. |

The HORNET threat model explicitly excludes targeted flow-confirmation attacks using traffic dynamics. Its evaluation measures a particular software-router implementation and comparison setup. Forwarding throughput from that study cannot be presented as VLP end-to-end goodput, handset performance, battery cost, or deployability; no such numbers are carried into this note. [R3] (§§2.2, 6)

Loopix also shows why “mix” must not be used as a synonym for a universally secure routing network. Its stated provider and participant assumptions are part of its result. A VLP proposal with open admission, arbitrary malicious providers, or a different cover schedule would need its own analysis. [R5] (§2.2)

## Directory, operator, and path trust

This section proposes questions for VLP; it does not import a particular network's policies.

**Directory and bootstrap.** Compare an owner-configured peer set, a signed directory, and distributed discovery. Record who binds a key to a service, who can add or remove relays, how freshness and rollback are handled, and which queries expose an intended destination. A correctly signed descriptor proves its relationship to a signing key, not the honesty, availability, or independent ownership of the described relay. Directory signatures alone cannot prevent an operator from running many identities or a lookup intermediary from withholding results.

**Sybil and operator independence.** Distinct node identifiers do not imply distinct operators, infrastructure providers, or observation domains. Research must state how membership is admitted and what a malicious operator can obtain by creating more identities. Open membership, private peer lists, and friend-based admission offer different reachability, governance, and adversary assumptions. A path across several servers under one operator must not be described as requiring independent organizations to collude.

**Guards and rotation.** Tor's guard design limits repeated exposure to newly sampled entry relays and includes resistance to attempts to force clients toward compromised guards. Its path rules constrain relay reuse, declared families, and network ranges. [R1] For VLP, investigate the opposite costs of persistent and frequently changed entries: a persistent entry can observe a client over time, whereas repeated fresh choices expand exposure. A compromised persistent entry remains a serious risk. Rotation caused by mobility or failures needs explicit reasoning; “choose the fastest new entry” is not a privacy policy.

**Network paths and autonomous systems.** Proposed operator diversity must be distinguished from diversity of IP networks, hosting providers, and autonomous systems (ASes) carrying the traffic. Apparent overlay disjointness does not demonstrate disjoint observation paths. Any research using inferred Internet routes should state uncertainty, route changes, asymmetric directions, and the age of the data. Selecting only on measured latency or capacity may concentrate traffic on a small set of providers; stricter diversity constraints may reduce available capacity and reachability. These are evaluation questions, not evidence for a VLP selection algorithm.

**Malicious failures and downgrade.** A relay can withhold service to influence path selection. The endpoint's intended privacy boundary should therefore be part of the future authenticated session and policy analysis. Failure of a layered route must not silently become permission to expose the client through a direct route. Research should compare explicit failure, a replacement meeting the same policy, and a separately authorized change in policy. Transport reachability does not establish user consent to a different trust model.

## Multipath, mobility, and useful traffic

Multipath is a transport and scheduling question as well as a path-trust question. It can be investigated for failover, capacity aggregation, or deadline-sensitive duplication. These goals are different from hiding the sender. More paths can expose traffic to more observers; correlated timing, shared identifiers, or duplicate messages can connect them. Several paths through the same operator are not independent privacy paths.

Before proposing striping, research needs to identify whether congestion control is per path or coupled, how reorder buffers are bounded, how scarce bandwidth is shared, and what happens when one path becomes slow. Endpoint protection needs an unambiguous relationship between keys, directions, epochs, packet identifiers, and paths. No packet should receive a new encryption invocation with a reused key/nonce pair, including when associated data changes. Re-sending a byte-identical cached ciphertext is a different operation from encrypting it again. Path changes do not relax these obligations.

| Use case | Comparison to investigate | Questions that determine suitability |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Server–server bulk transfer** | Direct protected channel; relay when routing or policy requires it; layered routing only for an identified privacy goal | Useful end-to-end goodput, forwarding capacity, total egress, bottleneck fairness, endpoint identity, and whether hiding either server is actually required |
| **Server–phone interactive traffic** | Direct and relayed endpoint channels under the same trust policy | Address changes, NAT state, network switching, sleep/wake, radio wakeups, high RTT, loss, reconnect costs, and whether continuity creates a long-lived observable identifier |
| **Phone–phone content through a service** | Inner phone-to-phone protection, with the service's forwarding or storage role stated separately | Key discovery and authentication, recipient availability, service metadata, recovery, delivery acknowledgments, and which party can decrypt attachments or messages |
| **Privacy-sensitive asynchronous messages** | Layered forwarding versus an explicit mixing/provider service | Acceptable delivery deadline, honest participation assumptions, mailbox linkage, cover budget, offline retention, duplicate handling, and recipient-authenticated content |

An asynchronous receipt might mean accepted by a provider, stored, delivered to an endpoint, or processed by the application. These meanings are not interchangeable. Retries and acknowledgments also create observable traffic and require message-level deduplication decisions. None of these delivery semantics is chosen here.

## Relay enforcement and operational metadata

An opaque inner channel is compatible with restricting what a relay will forward. Proposed relay interfaces need to identify the authorized peer, permitted next-hop or destination scope, resource accounting, expiration, and failure behavior without granting payload-decryption access merely for convenience. The enforcing relay or server must make these decisions; a client-supplied assertion is not enforcement.

If an exit resolves names or opens connections, research must cover the DNS disclosure point, address changes during resolution, destination authorization, and unauthorized access to private or administrative services. If the design is only a transit service, it should not accidentally gain arbitrary exit behavior. Admission tokens and stable accounting identifiers also belong in the privacy analysis because they may link sessions even when forwarding keys rotate.

Record metadata outside packet payloads: directory lookups, client registration, control messages, connection identifiers, mailbox identifiers, metrics labels, tracing, logs, and retry signals. Proposed observability should be sufficient to investigate resource and reliability problems while documenting precisely which relationships are retained and who can combine them. Deleting a packet field is insufficient if the same relationship appears in an operational log.

## Evidence needed before selecting an architecture

This is a proposed addition to the questions in the [validation plan](validation-plan.md). No new experiment was executed for this note.

1. **Define the claim and observer.** Distinguish a local passive observer, an AS-level observer, observation of all relevant links, a malicious relay, colluding operators, a malicious directory, a Sybil population, and a compromised endpoint. State the observable information and powers for each. “Global observer resistance” without a traffic model, honest-party assumptions, and a defined privacy objective is not an acceptable result.
2. **Trace metadata and policy.** For each candidate model, document every role's keys, addresses, identifiers, routing instructions, persistent state, and failure outputs. Include application authentication, bootstrap, provider retrieval, operational logging, and the effect of combining roles under one operator. Analyze policy-preserving recovery separately from a change in privacy policy.
3. **Specify comparable costs.** A future authorized evaluation should report end-to-end useful bytes and completion/latency distributions alongside CPU, memory, handset energy, setup cost, and connection scale. Count headers, retransmissions, duplicate paths, padding, cover, and forwarding bytes across all hops. A per-hop crypto or packet-forwarding microbenchmark is not a substitute for these measurements.
4. **Define adverse conditions.** The eventual scope should include delay, loss, reordering, constrained MTU, network switching, sleep/wake, route failure, slow recipients, dishonest forwarding, stale discovery information, and exhausted resource budgets. Distinguish what the system detects from what it merely times out. Explain which faults invalidate the privacy model itself.
5. **Design privacy evaluation before observing results.** Define traffic populations, ground truth, baselines, observation windows, attacker knowledge, false-positive costs, and uncertainty for any future correlation or classification study. Report performance and privacy under the same declared workload. Do not substitute successful decryption, random-looking bytes, or a larger relay count for anonymity evidence.
6. **Record what remains unproven.** Any proposed novelty should identify the exact difference from prior art and what would falsify its claimed benefit. Source review can justify a research question; it cannot establish new composition security, Internet compatibility, or production readiness. Experimental work remains subject to the recorded gate sequence and Implementation Approval.

Open decisions include whether VLP needs a publicly accessible relay network at all, which party's identity or destination it aims to hide, what operator-independence assumptions are acceptable, whether anonymous interactive traffic and private asynchronous messages are separate services, and what bandwidth, delay, and handset energy costs users would accept. These questions need evidence and explicit dispositions before formal specification work.
